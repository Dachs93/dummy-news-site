The tools you build on have a business model, and that model shapes what gets built, what stays free and what might change. Knowing the common patterns helps you choose tools with your eyes open.

## Per-seat pricing

You pay per developer per month. Common for editors, code hosting and collaboration tools.

- **Predictable** for buyers and sellers.
- **Incentive:** features that make each seat more valuable, plus admin controls large companies require.

## Usage-based pricing

You pay for what you consume: build minutes, requests, gigabytes, tokens.

- **Low barrier** to start; costs scale with success.
- **Risk:** surprise bills. Look for spending caps and alerts before you commit.

## Open core

The core is open source; paid features target larger organizations.

```text
Free / open source     Team                Enterprise
- core features        - hosted service    - SSO / SAML
- self-hosting         - support           - audit logs
- community help       - usage analytics   - compliance features
```

Single sign-on, audit logs and fine-grained permissions are classic paid features: individuals rarely need them, companies must have them.

## Hosted open source

The software is fully open; the company sells running it for you — upgrades, backups, scaling and support.

## Why it matters to you

- **Free tiers are marketing.** They're valuable, but limits can tighten. Know your exit path.
- **Licenses can change.** Several popular projects have moved from permissive to more restrictive licenses as companies looked for sustainable revenue. Check the license before you build a business on a tool.
- **Self-hosting is leverage.** If you can run it yourself, a pricing change is an inconvenience rather than an emergency.
- **Pay for what you rely on.** A tool you depend on whose company can't sustain itself is a long-term risk.

## A simple test

Before adopting a tool, ask: who pays for this, and what happens to me if they stop? The answer doesn't have to be "nobody" — it just has to be one you're comfortable with.
